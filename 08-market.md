# Part VIII: Market

## A Note on Method

Every figure in this chapter that is not explicitly FreClean's own statement is sourced to a named third-party publisher, listed in full in [Sources and References](../../sources/REFERENCES.md). Where sources disagree, which they do substantially in this industry, this chapter states the range rather than picking whichever number best supports an optimistic narrative.

## Global Household & Commercial Cleaning Products Market

Six independent market-research publishers, surveyed for this book in 2026, size the global household cleaning products market for 2025 at figures ranging from approximately **USD 41.7 billion to USD 282.3 billion**, with forecast CAGRs clustering between roughly 4.4% and 6.5% through the early-to-mid 2030s. **(Confirmed, third-party sourced; see References for all six citations.)** The wide range reflects differing product-scope definitions (some include only household surface/laundry cleaners; others include a broader "cleaning products" category spanning commercial and industrial use) rather than a single agreed-upon market size. Coherent Market Insights specifically breaks out a commercial-end-user segment at approximately USD 92.3 billion for 2025 within its broader USD 163.45 billion cleaning-products estimate: relevant because FreClean's business spans both household and commercial customers (Part V).

## TAM (Total Addressable Market)

Using the broadest reasonable interpretation, the full global cleaning products and services category, the addressable market runs into the hundreds of billions of dollars annually. That is a standard, honest TAM statement for the category FreClean operates in, but it is not a meaningful planning number for an early-stage, single-country company, and this chapter treats that limitation as important rather than glossing over it.

## SAM (Serviceable Addressable Market)

Narrowing to FreClean's actually-stated geography (Haiti, with expansion intent into the Dominican Republic and the wider Caribbean) and its actually-stated segments (residential, commercial/hospitality, and institutional cleaning, plus a manufactured product line): no third-party publisher in this book's source list breaks out a Haiti- or Caribbean-specific cleaning products/services market size. **(TBD: no credible country-level or regional figure was found; this book declines to estimate one by simple population-scaling of a global figure, since that method is not reliable enough to present as a market size.)**

What can be stated with a citation: Haiti's population is approximately 11.9–12.0 million (2025 estimates, World Bank and Trading Economics), and Haiti's nominal GDP is estimated at roughly USD 24–34 billion depending on methodology and source year (see References for the range and the reason for it). These are demographic and macroeconomic anchors, not a cleaning-market size: presented here as the honest limit of what can be sourced, not as a proxy SAM.

## The Haiti and Caribbean Opportunity

Two sourced, non-cleaning-specific facts are directly relevant to FreClean's specific positioning:

- **Diaspora capital:** Haiti's diaspora remitted approximately USD 3.8–4.1 billion in 2023–2024: equivalent to roughly 20% of Haiti's GDP (IHSI, via The Haitian Times; Trading Economics; see References). This is a large, recurring capital flow into the Haitian economy from outside it, structurally distinct from domestic consumer spending power.
- **Domestic economic pressure:** Haiti's real GDP contracted for a seventh consecutive year in 2025 (-2.7%), with inflation averaging 28.3% and an estimated 49.0% of the population living on less than US$3.00/day (World Bank; see References). This constrains domestic discretionary spending on non-essential goods and services.

Read together, these two data points suggest that FreClean's near-term domestic Haitian opportunity may be more constrained by macroeconomic conditions than a simple population or GDP figure alone would suggest, while a diaspora-linked opportunity (remittance-funded spending, diaspora investment, or diaspora-directed digital payments) may be a structurally distinct and potentially more resilient near-term opportunity: a connection this book raises as an analytical observation, not a confirmed FreClean strategy (see Part II).
